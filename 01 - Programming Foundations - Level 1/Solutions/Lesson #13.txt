1️⃣ Why is asking “Which programming language is better?” an incomplete question? (Using the color or car analogy)
Asking which language is better is incomplete because context is everything. Just like color means nothing without context—As an example for me, preferring a black suit while disliking black cars—or how asking for the "best car" depends entirely on whether I need speed (a Lamborghini), off-road driving (a 4x4), heavy loads (a truck), or fuel efficiency (a compact car), a programming language can only be evaluated based on where and how it is used. There is no single "best" language overall.

2️⃣ What is the correct answer to “Which language is better?” What does the phrase “It depends on your needs” really mean in programming?
The correct answer is always “It depends on your needs.” In programming, this means choosing a language based on your use case + ecosystem—such as the specific domain, performance requirements, team skills, available libraries, and hosting constraints.

3️⃣ Why do many programming languages exist?
Many languages exist because different problems require different trade-offs. Three major factors that make languages suitable for different situations are:

- Speed and Performance: How fast the code executes and how close it is to the hardware.

- Safety and Tooling: The level of type checking, error prevention, and developer tools available.

- Ecosystem: The availability of pre-built libraries, frameworks, and community support for specific domains (like web dev, AI, or embedded systems).

4️⃣ What’s the difference between programming and a programming language? (Using the carpentry analogy)
Just like a carpenter uses tools like a hammer, saw, and screwdriver, a programmer uses a programming language as a tool to write instructions. However, knowing how to use tools for ten minutes doesn't make someone a carpenter; building furniture requires the craft, skill, practice, creativity, and structural design. Similarly, a programming language is just the tool, while programming itself is the craft of designing clear, logical solutions.

5️⃣ Why should you focus on learning programming fundamentals before languages? What part is the “craft,” and what part is just “tools”?
I should focus on fundamentals because syntax (the tools) can be learned in a very short time, while 95% of programming—such as problem-solving, data structures, algorithms, architecture, debugging, and system design—makes up the actual craft. Fundamentals transfer across any language I will ever use.

6️⃣ Why is it not a good idea to start with modern languages? (Using the driving analogy)
Starting with modern languages full of ready-made functions and automatic tools gives me the false illusion of being a skilled programmer, much like learning to drive using park assist, cameras, and sensors. Just as a driver must first learn manual parking using mirrors and judgment to truly master control, a programmer must learn foundational concepts from the ground up without relying on heavy abstractions as a crutch.

7️⃣ Why is C++ recommended as the first language to learn?
C++ is recommeded because:

1. It builds rock-solid programming fundamentals and teaches me core concepts.

2. It shows me how computers truly work under the hood regarding memory, logic, and performance.

3. It trains my mind to think like a real engineer, giving me the key to pick up any other modern language quickly and easily.

8️⃣ What does “C++ = Salary ++” mean?
It means that learning C++ is a powerful long-term investment in my career. Even if my daily job doesn't require writing C++, the deep mental training, hardware understanding, and problem-solving mindset it builds make me a much more valuable and adaptable engineer across any technical domain.

9️⃣ What makes a good programmer? Is it the language they use, or something deeper?
A good programmer is defined by something much deeper than their choice of language: their ability to think clearly, break down large problems logically, manage trade-offs, and master the craft of problem-solving rather than defending tool preferences or falling for fanboy wars.
